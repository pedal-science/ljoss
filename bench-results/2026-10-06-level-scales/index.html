What the levels mean

The app shows numbers that come from namchain: a peak and an RMS in dBFS for the device's input, every node in a chain, and the device's output. This is what those numbers are worth — measured by playing known signals out of an SSL 2+ Mk II into the pedal (one output looped straight back as an analogue reference, one into the pedal), polling engine.levels at 20 Hz, and recording the pedal's own converters at the same time so every meter can be checked against the audio it was measuring.

The short answer

The engine's numbers are accurate: over the linear range its reported RMS matches the RMS of the pedal's own converter stream to within 0.06 dB, and a chain's node levels follow the rig's own gains to a median of 0.00 dB. The unit is not the problem.

The reference is. This pedal's input does not clip at 0 dBFS — it clips at -11.2 dBFS, because the codec's analogue input runs out of headroom at about 2.7 dBu while the interface's line output can deliver +14.5 dBu. A meter that draws 0 dBFS as the top of the scale is therefore showing roughly 11.2 dB of headroom that does not exist, and a player reading "−6 dBFS" is looking at an input that is already clipping.

What the scale should be

  • Input meter: show headroom to the input's own clip point, not to 0 dBFS. The maths is headroom = level_dbfs - clip_dbfs, with clip_dbfs measured for the device rather than assumed (here -11.2 dBFS, or 2.7 dBu at the jack). A scale that runs from −60 dB to the clip point, with the region above it marked as overload, says the true thing at a glance.
  • Nodes: dBFS is right, and the meter must not clamp at 0. A node's output is a float and is routinely past full scale; what clips is the DAC. Mark the device output's 0 dBFS on the same scale so "this node is hot" and "this is about to clip the output" are different statements.
  • Output meter: pair the peak with a loudness number. At the same played peak the shapes below differ by 18.8 dB in loudness. Peak answers "will it clip"; only a K-weighted loudness (LUFS) or an A-weighted level answers "how loud is it".
  • Say what the floor is. The engine's RMS meter cannot read below about −78 dBFS per sample (its accumulator rounds each sample's square to an integer at 226), so it reports silence where the converter still has 1.8 dB of noise. A meter should draw a floor it can actually measure, or say "below the meter".

The input scale, measured

One signal, four landmarks, all in the engine's own dBFS. The interface's own ADC confirms the level that left it (its loopback reads -10.37 dB), and the pedal's ADC reads 11.78 dB above the digital level played, which is what puts the clip point at -11.2 dBFS rather than 0.

Same peak, different loudness

Every signal here was played at a peak of −24 dBFS into the pedal, and measured at its output through the interface's input. The spread between the quietest and the loudest is the reason a peak meter cannot be the only thing on the meter: two signals the meter calls identical differ by more than 20 dB in what a listener hears.
